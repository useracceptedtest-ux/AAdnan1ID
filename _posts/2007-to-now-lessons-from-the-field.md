When I finished my Bachelor of Informatics in 2007, I thought the hard part was over. Exams were hard. Getting the degree was hard. So what could possibly be harder than that?

Everything.

Not the technical work, necessarily — though that had its own surprises. What was hard was everything the textbooks never mentioned: how to actually get things done in a company, how to be useful when you're the newest person in the room, how to tell the difference between being busy and being effective.

I'm not writing this as a success story. There were years that were difficult. But if I could hand eighteen years of hindsight to a younger version of myself — or to you, just starting out — this is what I'd say.

## Learn how to learn, not just how to code

The single most useful thing I learned: **the industry will keep changing, so your value is in your ability to absorb change, not in any specific technology.**

In 2007 the stack I learned in school was good for a while. Then it wasn't. And then it wasn't. And then it wasn't again. Every few years the thing you're an expert in becomes a legacy system someone has to maintain for the next decade.

Languages, frameworks, cloud providers, tools — all of it has a shelf life. Some of what I'm using daily was invented after I graduated. What hasn't changed:

- How to read documentation when the docs are bad
- How to break a vague problem into smaller problems
- How to verify your own work
- How to ask a good question

Get good at those and a new tool is a few weeks of inconvenience, not a career crisis.

## Learn the business, not just the system

Nobody in school taught me this, and it took me years to notice: **users do not care about the technology.** They care about whether their problem went away.

I have seen technically excellent projects fail because they solved a problem nobody had. And I've seen ugly, boring, unglamorous fixes succeed because they addressed what people actually needed. The second kind of work is much more common, and it's much more valuable.

Learn your users. Learn what they do all day. Learn what annoys them. Learn which annoying thing would make them relieved if it disappeared. Then build that. The rest is implementation.

## Document like your future self is a stranger

Your memory will fail you. It will fail you sooner than you expect.

Every non-trivial thing worth knowing should end up in writing somewhere outside your head — a comment, a wiki page, a commit message, a README. I've inherited systems built by brilliant people who left six months ago and took their entire understanding with them. Don't be that person. Write down why, not just what. The "what" is in the code; the "why" is the only thing you can save them.

## Ask for help earlier than feels comfortable

This one I'm still working on.

Early on I spent hours — sometimes days — trying to solve things alone because I didn't want to look incompetent in front of senior colleagues. That was a mistake. Hours of silent struggle is a much worse signal than a quick question.

Say: "I've been stuck on this for two hours, here's what I've tried, here's where I think the problem is." That's not weakness. That's a competent engineer giving a useful status update. Senior people respond well to it. It also teaches you faster than grinding does.

## Being right is less valuable than being clear

I've been technically right in meetings and watched the wrong decision get made anyway. Being right matters less than you want it to. What matters more:

- Saying it plainly, in plain language
- Knowing your audience's concerns before you present
- Framing the tradeoff, not just your preference
- Not being attached to a specific solution when a better one shows up

Being right and being useful are different skills. Aim for useful.

## Quiet competence compounds; visible performance does not

The people I trust most as colleagues are the ones who consistently do good work and say very little about it. Nothing kills a good reputation faster than a person who talks constantly about how much they know.

Be excellent, be dependable, help people without announcing it. That reputation outlives any presentation you ever gave, and it's the reason people will vouch for you years later.

## Take care of yourself and your reputation

Reputation in this field is built over decades and destroyed in an afternoon. Be helpful, be honest about what you don't know, and be careful how you treat people who can do nothing for you — because in a small industry, that's most of them.

Also, and I cannot stress this enough: **don't let work be the only thing in your life.** I've seen people burn out completely chasing a title or a salary that never felt like enough. Nobody at the end wishes they'd worked more hours. Keep the parts of your life that aren't about your job intact.

## You don't have to know it all

Nineteen years ago, fresh out of university, I felt like a fraud who would be exposed any day. I wasn't. Nobody was judging me the way I thought — most people were too busy with their own work to evaluate a junior's knowledge, and the ones who weren't busy were the ones willing to help.

If you're new in the industry, understand this: **people generally want you to succeed.** The fear you feel about being found out is mostly a bug in your own head. Ask questions. Take the task nobody wants. Do it well.

You already have the hard part done — you cared enough to start. That's more than most people manage.
